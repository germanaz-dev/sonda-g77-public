The Imputed Sovereign: 1907 and the Commercial Wire

Yesterday’s analysis of the Starlink operational cycle documented an institutional maneuver: SpaceX’s leadership retroactively justified battlefield bandwidth throttling as an administrative enforcement of a private consumer contract—a standard “Terms of Service” violation. By transmuting an act of kinetic battlefield regulation into the enforcement of an Acceptable Use Policy, the commercial monopoly insulated itself behind the corporate veil, treating a dependent sovereign fighting an existential war as an ordinary non-compliant subscriber.

This structural friction—between the kinetic necessities of sovereign belligerents and the private contractual prerogatives of commercial communications monopolies—is not an anomaly of the orbital age. Humanity encountered this exact institutional impasse at the turn of the twentieth century, during the transition from state-operated postal systems to private global telecommunications cartels.

Between June and October 1907, delegates from forty-four nations convened in the Netherlands for the Second Hague Peace Conference. Among the pressing technological problems before them was the rapid proliferation of privately owned submarine telegraph cables (dominated by the British Eastern Telegraph Company cartel) and the aggressive expansion of the Marconi International Marine Communication Company. Marconi had instituted a corporate policy known as the “non-intercourse rule”: under Marconi’s standard commercial lease agreements, private and naval vessels equipped with Marconi wireless apparatus were contractually forbidden from exchanging messages with coastal stations or ships utilizing rival systems (such as Germany’s Telefunken), except in cases of verified maritime distress. Marconi defended this restrictive practice as a legitimate exercise of private contract law and the protection of proprietary technical infrastructure.

At the 1907 conference, the delegates recognized a lethal systemic danger: if private communications companies were allowed to invoke commercial charters, contractual neutrality, or corporate terms of service to dictate which messages they transmitted, throttled, or refused during an international conflict, private corporations would wield the de facto kinetic prerogatives of sovereign belligerents, yet remain entirely free from the legal accountability imposed by the laws of war. A private cable monopoly could sever communications to one belligerent on grounds of commercial risk or contractual non-compliance, effectively determining the outcome of a siege or a naval campaign while disclaiming public responsibility.

The institutional response constructed at The Hague was codified in Convention (V) respecting the Rights and Duties of Neutral Powers and Persons in War on Land (1907). The drafters first established a commercial permissive baseline in Article 8: a neutral power is not obligated to forbid or restrict a belligerent’s use of telegraph, telephone, or wireless installations belonging to “companies or private individuals.” Belligerent states were legally permitted to purchase operational communication services from private commercial vendors on the open market.

Crucially, however, the delegates enacted an immediate structural interlock in Article 9: “Every measure of restriction or prohibition taken by a neutral Power in regard to the matters referred to in Articles 7 and 8 must be impartially applied by it to both belligerents. A neutral Power shall see to the same obligation being observed by companies or private individuals owning telegraph or telephone cables or wireless telegraphy apparatus.”

Article 9 established the doctrine of imputed sovereign responsibility over dual-use commercial infrastructure. The treaty pierced the corporate veil of the communications monopoly. Under public international law, a private infrastructure provider was strictly barred from using its private corporate discretion, commercial contracts, or terms of service to unilaterally throttle or restrict service to an active belligerent. If any restriction was imposed, the sovereign state where that company was chartered or located bore an affirmative legal obligation under the law of nations to enforce absolute impartiality—either forbidding the private throttling entirely or ensuring that identical operational restrictions were applied across all combatants.

In the subsequent decades, this doctrine atrophied not because it was legally repudiated, but because twentieth-century total war bypassed it. During the First and Second World Wars, states resolved the ambiguity by outright nationalization: Great Britain severed enemy submarine cables and placed commercial cable landings under the Electra House military censorship apparatus in 1914; Woodrow Wilson nationalized Western Union, the Postal Telegraph Cable Company, and the Bell telephone system in July 1918 by joint congressional resolution.

The modern crisis exposed on the Ukrainian front is the structural consequence of privatizing this space without preserving the 1907 public-law leash. In the post-Cold War era, militaries abandoned dedicated, state-built communications constellations in favor of commercial off-the-shelf procurement to reduce capital expenditure. Client states procured terminals through commercial aid packages and consumer accounts governed by click-through End User License Agreements, rather than government-to-government defense treaties. Meanwhile, the patron state where the infrastructure monopoly is incorporated has quietly abdicated its Article 9 duty of oversight. By allowing the platform owner to claim private contractual autonomy, the patron preserves plausible diplomatic deniability, while the commercial monopolist exercises raw geopolitical leverage under the mundane guise of acceptable-use enforcement.
