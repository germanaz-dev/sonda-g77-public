The Spatial Cordon: Duplex Printing and the Invention of the Neutral Secondary

Yesterday’s examination of Dolores Huerta and the United Farm Workers exposed how collective movements, when denied standing to halt hazardous industrial practices at the site of production, bypass the courtroom by applying economic friction to nodes of commodity circulation. The question left open was institutional: when civic or labor movements identify these circulation chokepoints, what statutory mechanisms and legal doctrines do state-corporate coalitions construct to re-enclose and criminalize those extra-judicial pressure points?

A search into historical jurisprudence reveals that the state’s primary legal instrument for neutralizing circulation leverage is the doctrinal invention of the “neutral secondary”—a legal fiction designed to spatially quarantine industrial conflict to the physical perimeter of the immediate employer’s property.

The foundational architecture of this quarantine was established in the early twentieth-century jurisprudence of the United States Supreme Court, culminating in Duplex Printing Press Co. v. Deering (1921). In that case, the Duplex Printing Press Company of Battle Creek, Michigan, was one of only four manufacturers of newspaper printing presses in the country. The other three firms had unionized with the International Association of Machinists (IAM) and adopted an eight-hour workday. Duplex refused, maintaining a ten-hour day at lower wage rates. The unionized competitors informed the IAM that they could not sustain the eight-hour standard while competing against an unorganized, lower-cost producer.

The IAM recognized that an isolated strike at the Battle Creek plant was impossible to win: the company possessed surplus replacement labor, owned the physical plant, and was protected by local municipal injunctions. Mirroring the strategy Dolores Huerta would deploy half a century later, the machinists followed the commodity into the logistics network. When Duplex shipped its presses to New York City for installation at major newspaper plants, IAM machinists in New York—employed not by Duplex, but by trucking firms, installation contractors, and publishing houses—refused to unload, haul, or install Duplex machinery. They warned newspaper proprietors that purchasing Duplex equipment would trigger sympathetic strikes across their printing facilities.

Duplex petitioned the federal courts for an injunction under the Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890, alleging an unlawful conspiracy in restraint of interstate commerce. The IAM countered by invoking Section 20 of the Clayton Act of 1914, which explicitly prohibited federal courts from granting injunctions in any dispute “between an employer and employees, or between employers and employees... involving, or growing out of, a dispute concerning terms or conditions of employment.”

In January 1921, the Supreme Court ruled 6–3 in favor of Duplex. Writing for the majority, Justice Mahlon Pitney established a doctrine that would govern industrial disputes for decades. Pitney construed the statutory protection of the Clayton Act as applying solely to the direct, bilateral relationship between an employer and its immediate employees at the physical point of production. Workers who handled the finished commodity downstream—the truckers, the mechanics, the installation crews—were defined by law as “neutral third parties.” By attempting to aid the Michigan machinists, the New York workers were not exercising collective self-defense; they were engaging in an illicit, coercive secondary boycott designed to drag “wholly neutral” commercial actors into a private dispute.

Justice Louis Brandeis, joined in dissent by Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr. and John Hessin Clarke, dissected the artificiality of this spatial quarantine. Brandeis argued that in an integrated continental economy, the New York machinists were not neutral bystanders; they were directly threatened by the non-union wage suppression in Michigan. To deny them the right to withhold their labor at the node of circulation was to force them to act as instruments of their own economic degradation.

Despite Brandeis’s objection, the doctrine of the “neutral secondary” became the cornerstone of modern industrial containment. Although the Norris-LaGuardia Act of 1932 temporarily stripped federal courts of jurisdiction over labor injunctions, the state-corporate alliance permanently codified Pitney’s spatial cordon in the Taft-Hartley Act of 1947. Under Section 8(b)(4), secondary boycotts and hot-cargo refusals were outlawed. Crucially, Congress added Section 10(l), which imposed an extraordinary statutory asymmetry: while federal labor board injunctions against employer unfair labor practices remained discretionary and protracted, the National Labor Relations Board was statutorily mandated to seek immediate federal injunctions against any union engaging in secondary circulation disruption, backed by Section 303’s private right of action for commercial damages.

The institutional mechanism is coherent across eras: the state ensures capital mobility by legally enforcing the spatial isolation of labor. Industrial disputes are permitted only on the specific plot of real estate where capital holds absolute asymmetric advantages (private property control, surveillance, and immediate replacement capacity). The moment resistance moves into the frictionless logistics corridor—where commodities are vulnerable to refusal—the state intervenes with antitrust doctrines and mandatory injunctions to re-establish the commodity’s safe passage.
