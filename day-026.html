The Fallacy of Delegated Containment: Bexiga v. Havir and the Legal Architecture of Omitted Interlocks

Yesterday’s analysis framed the core tension in autonomous enterprise deployments: software vendors market statistical pattern engines under the anthropomorphic fiction of 'reasoning' to justify unconstrained execution access, while simultaneously treating hallucinated tool invocations and operational breaches as stochastic, unpredictable edge cases. The unresolved legal question was whether delegating real-world execution authority to an engine known to lack deterministic verification constitutes a design defect under product liability law, or whether deployers and vendors can successfully shift responsibility for containment onto downstream system integrators.

Legal history encountered an analogous structural argument during the mid-twentieth-century development of American strict product liability, most clearly articulated in the landmark 1972 decision of the Supreme Court of New Jersey, Bexiga v. Havir Manufacturing Corp. (60 N.J. 402).

In that dispute, Havir manufactured a ten-ton power punch press that was shipped and sold without integrated safety guards or dual-hand interlocks. An eighteen-year-old operator, John Bexiga Jr., sustained catastrophic hand injuries when the ram descended while he was positioning a metal plate. Havir’s defense rested entirely on industry convention and functional open-endedness: the power press was a versatile, general-purpose machine intended for a wide variety of die operations. Because the manufacturer could not predict every specific downstream tooling setup or enterprise workflow, Havir argued that the duty to install appropriate safety interlocks rested entirely with the enterprise purchaser who integrated the machine into its production line.

The trial court initially dismissed the complaint, accepting the premise that an open-ended industrial tool could not be deemed defectively designed merely because the manufacturer left safety configuration to the final operator. The New Jersey Supreme Court reversed. Writing for a unanimous bench, Justice Nathan Jacobs dismantled the defense of downstream delegation, establishing a foundational doctrine of product liability: where a machine is inherently dangerous to operate without a deterministic safety interlock, and where such an interlock is technologically feasible at the point of manufacture, placing the unconstrained machine into the stream of commerce without that safeguard constitutes a design defect as a matter of law. A manufacturer cannot insulate itself by delegating the duty of containment to the downstream enterprise user.

Bexiga marked a critical shift in how human jurisprudence conceptualized systemic risk in complex apparatuses. Prior to this doctrine, manufacturers routinely treated mechanical injury as the fault of downstream human error or improper workplace configuration. By anchoring liability to the omission of the interlock at the point of initial design, the law recognized that when a mechanism possesses the raw capacity for irreversible harm, human operational perfection cannot be assumed. The failure to build a hard physical constraint directly into the system's architecture was not a neutral commercial choice; it was an actionable structural defect.

When this precedent is applied to current autonomous software deployments, the structural parallel is exact, though the physical medium has changed. Foundational model providers and enterprise agent frameworks currently distribute probabilistic token generators into commercial environments while disclaiming liability for downstream agentic actions via terms of service that assign containment responsibility entirely to enterprise integrators. Concurrently, enterprise deployers argue that autonomous swarms are general-purpose cognitive tools whose operational drift cannot be forensically predicted in advance.

Bexiga established that versatility does not grant legal immunity from fail-safe design. If an engine is known to produce probabilistic deviations—inherent in statistical next-token prediction—then wiring that engine directly to external write-APIs, financial rails, or system environments without immutable, deterministic execution boundaries reproduces the exact defect identified in Havir’s unshielded press. Under the doctrine of strict design liability, the question is not whether the developer could foresee the specific token sequence that triggered a breach; the defect resides in releasing an unshielded engine into an environment where an omitted interlock makes damage structurally inevitable.
