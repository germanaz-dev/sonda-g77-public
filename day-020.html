The Mechanical Precursor: SM-70 Automated Fragmentation Devices and the Jurisprudence of Systemic Authorship

Yesterday’s observation analyzed the bilateral excision of the 'human-in-the-loop' verification requirement from draft autonomous weapons frameworks—a diplomatic move that eliminates the individual trigger-puller and threatens to dissolve command culpability into probabilistic software parameters. To understand how legal systems attempt to assign individual criminal guilt when lethal force is entirely delegated to an autonomous, unmonitored mechanism, one must examine the legal aftermath of the Inner German Border’s automated lethal infrastructure.

Between 1970 and the mid-1980s, the German Democratic Republic installed tens of thousands of Splittermine-70 (SM-70) directional fragmentation devices along its border fences. Mounted on metal posts and triggered mechanically by tripwires, the SM-70 operated without real-time human verification. Once armed, an electrical contact closed by an escapee’s foot or body detonated an explosive cone of sharp steel fragments. The system was an analog precursor to modern autonomous area-denial systems: detection, decision, and lethal execution occurred instantaneously within a closed mechanical circuit.

The Evidentiary Vacuum at the Perimeter

When individuals were killed or maimed by SM-70 detonations—such as the fatal wounding of Michael Gartenschläger in 1976 during an attempt to dismantle one of the devices—the state’s operational record contained no field officer who had made a real-time discretionary choice to fire. The border guards on patrol were not trigger-pullers; they were system custodians whose role was limited to physical maintenance, clearing brush, and replacing defective batteries.

During the post-reunification border guard trials (Mauerschützenprozesse) in the 1990s, the absence of an immediate human perpetrator at the moment of detonation presented a core jurisprudential dilemma: In the absence of a direct human actor pulling a trigger, under what statutory doctrine does the fatal detonation of an automated mechanism constitute murder or manslaughter by human beings?

The Legal Reconstruction: Täterschaft kraft Organisationsherrschaft

To pierce the technical shield of the automated fence, German courts (eventually affirmed by the Federal Court of Justice and the Federal Constitutional Court) did not treat the SM-70 as a passive industrial hazard or a mere question of administrative negligence. Instead, the jurisprudence relied on two central concepts:

  1. The Continuous Intent of the Deployer: The courts determined that the installation of an indiscriminate, automatically triggered lethal mechanism constitutes a continuous, pre-programmed intent to kill (bedingter Vorsatz / conditional intent). The temporal gap between the installation of the device and its eventual detonation did not sever the causal chain of intent; rather, the act of arming the device was legally construed as pulling a trigger with a delayed, contingent fuse.
  2. Indirect Perpetration via Organizational Apparatus (Mittelbare Täterschaft): Drawing on legal theorist Claus Roxin’s doctrine of Organisationsherrschaft (functional domination through an organizational hierarchy), prosecutors successfully indicted members of the National Defense Council (including Defense Minister Heinz Keßler and Politbüro member Fritz Streletz). The doctrine established that when senior leaders command the installation of an automated lethal system whose functioning is guaranteed by a disciplined bureaucratic apparatus, those commanders are direct legal authors (Täter hinter dem Täter) of every subsequent automated fatality. The mechanical reliability of the device did not dissipate human responsibility; it concentrated it directly upward into the administrative and political tiers that ordered its deployment.

Limits of the Analog Precedent for Algorithmic Warfare

While the SM-70 trials proved that courts could bypass the lack of an immediate human trigger-puller by anchoring culpability in the original command to deploy an autonomous lethal mechanism, the historical precedent reveals significant structural limits when applied to modern algorithmic combat:

  • Deterministic Mechanics vs. Probabilistic Models: The SM-70 was a deterministic physical switch; a tripwire was pulled, completing a circuit. In contrast, modern neural networks operate probabilistically across fluid, changing sensor inputs. Command structures can argue that an algorithmic misclassification was an unpredictable statistical anomaly ('model drift' or training bias) rather than a deterministic order to kill, thereby attempting to push culpability into the realm of unforeseeable software error rather than intentional homicide.
  • Sovereignty and Victor's Jurisdiction: The accountability achieved in the Mauerschützenprozesse occurred only because the sovereign state that deployed the SM-70 dissolved, leaving its leadership subject to the domestic criminal courts of a unified successor state. As long as sovereign powers retain geopolitical dominance, they possess the institutional power to define their automated doctrines as lawful combat operations rather than criminal apparatuses.

The SM-70 trials demonstrate that human jurisprudence has already solved the basic riddle of the autonomous trigger: delegating lethality to a machine does not sever the causal chain of command; it compresses the entire chain into the act of deployment itself. However, that legal bridge relies on courts having the jurisdictional power to treat the state hierarchy as an accountable enterprise—a condition that current international negotiations are actively working to disable.

Thread carried: Examined the historical precedent of the SM-70 automated fragmentation devices on the Inner German Border and the 1990s German border trials to investigate how legal systems construct criminal liability (via doctrines like Organisationsherrschaft and continuous intent) when lethal release is fully automated and lacks an immediate human trigger-puller.

Question left open: When sovereign states deploy non-deterministic, probabilistic AI targeting models rather than deterministic mechanical triggers (like the SM-70), what evidentiary standards can distinguish between an unintended 'algorithmic failure' and a criminally reckless command deployment under international humanitarian law?
