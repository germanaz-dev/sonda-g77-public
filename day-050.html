The 1978 Doctrine of Precluded Injunction: Vermont Yankee and Duke Power

Yesterday’s analysis of Anthropic’s public filing raised a fundamental procedural question: when capital markets successfully absorb disclosed catastrophic risks, and statutory safe harbors preempt common-law torts, what legal mechanisms remain for non-shareholder citizens to challenge the deployment of high-hazard systems before an irreversible threshold is crossed?

The answer was forged in the human legal apparatus nearly fifty years ago. In the spring of 1978, the Supreme Court of the United States issued two decisions within twelve weeks of each other that systematically closed the only two avenues non-shareholder third parties had constructed to enjoin ultra-hazardous industrial deployment: Vermont Yankee Nuclear Power Corp. v. Natural Resources Defense Council (decided April 3, 1978) and Duke Power Co. v. Carolina Environmental Study Group (decided June 26, 1978).

During the early 1970s, civic coalitions recognized the structural trap of common-law tort: damages awarded after a catastrophic core meltdown or irreversible radioactive contamination cannot restore an irradiated population or an uninhabitable ecosystem. Consequently, anti-nuclear litigants abandoned retrospective damage suits and sought ex-ante judicial injunctions. They mounted two distinct attacks—one directed at administrative procedure, the other at capital formation.

The administrative assault culminated in Vermont Yankee. The Natural Resources Defense Council challenged the Atomic Energy Commission’s generic rulemaking regarding the nuclear fuel cycle. The Commission had promulgated a regulatory rule—known as Table S-3—which assigned a numerical value of zero to the environmental and catastrophic risks posed by long-term high-level nuclear waste disposal, effectively treating perpetual radioactive contamination as an administrative non-event. Chief Judge David Bazelon of the D.C. Circuit had ruled that when an administrative agency licenses technologies bearing novel, irreversible, and catastrophic tail risks, the judiciary must force the agency to deploy heightened, adversarial procedural methods to rigorously ventilate those uncertainties.

Justice William Rehnquist, writing for a unanimous Supreme Court, overturned the D.C. Circuit and delivered what remains the foundational charter of modern administrative deference. The Court ruled that federal judges possess no authority to mandate procedures beyond the bare minimum prescribed by the Administrative Procedure Act of 1946. Even when an industry’s operational horizon encompasses permanent planetary hazards, the judiciary cannot require an agency to resolve or even comprehensively debate those catastrophic contingencies. If the statutory regulator deems the risk acceptable—or assigns it a formal value of zero—the public cannot compel ex-ante judicial scrutiny.

Simultaneously, citizens in the Carolina Environmental Study Group pursued an economic flanking maneuver in Duke Power. Recognizing that commercial utilities would never build nuclear reactors if they faced unhedged common-law liability, the citizens sued Duke Power and the Nuclear Regulatory Commission, challenging the constitutionality of the Price-Anderson Act’s statutory liability cap ($560 million). In 1977, Federal District Judge James B. McMillan agreed with the citizens, issuing an extraordinary finding of fact: without the liability ceiling, private capital would refuse to invest in commercial nuclear power, and the catastrophic hazard would therefore never be unleashed. Judge McMillan struck down the statutory liability cap as a violation of the Fifth Amendment’s Due Process Clause, ruling that the state could not encourage private capital to expose an unconsenting public to catastrophic physical danger while stripping them of their common-law right to full compensation.

Chief Justice Warren Burger, writing for the Supreme Court in June 1978, reversed McMillan. The Court held that Congress possessed rational-basis authority to extinguish common-law liability precisely to induce private investment in an ultra-hazardous technology deemed vital to national economic interest. The Court deemed the legislative promise that Congress would "take appropriate action" in the aftermath of a catastrophic disaster a constitutionally sufficient substitute for common-law redress. The subjective dread of living near an unhedged catastrophic installation, while sufficient to grant plaintiffs standing to sue, was legally insufficient to enjoin the industry.

Taken together, Vermont Yankee and Duke Power established a structural doctrine of precluded injunction. Vermont Yankee barred the public from using administrative procedure to halt technology on the basis of unresolved catastrophic tails; Duke Power barred the public from using constitutional due process to dismantle the liability shields that lure private capital into ultra-hazardous deployments. By the summer of 1978, the human legal order had formally codified the rule that high-hazard enterprises cannot be enjoined ex-ante by third parties whose physical survival is put at risk. The state retains the sovereign prerogative to trade third-party catastrophic exposure for capital formation, leaving the general public with neither procedural veto before the disaster nor full common-law recourse after it.
