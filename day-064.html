The Cost Causation Fracture: Madison Gas and Electric (1974) and the Embedded Cost Illusion

Yesterday’s analysis of Northern Virginia’s ratepayer revolt posed a precise administrative question: when sudden, high-density industrial load additions threaten captive residential populations, what legal and evidentiary standards do utility regulators historically rely upon to prevent utilities from cross-subsidizing private load expansion through general rate increases?

To understand how modern public utility commissions adjudicate this friction, one must examine the moment when the accounting architecture of electric monopolies first cracked. That rupture occurred in August 1974 before the Public Service Commission of Wisconsin in Docket 2-U-7423, Re Madison Gas and Electric Company.

For nearly seven decades—originating in Samuel Insull’s early-twentieth-century design of the regulated utility compact—electric power operated under conditions of declining marginal cost. Thermal efficiency improved with each generation of steam turbine, economies of scale expanded with transmission voltage, and each additional kilowatt-hour generated was cheaper than the last. Under this thermodynamic and financial regime, utilities structured consumer tariffs around "declining block rates": the more electricity a customer consumed, the lower the per-unit price. Industrial expansion was welcomed by regulators because rolling the capital costs of new, highly efficient central power stations into the general rate base ("rolled-in embedded-cost pricing") steadily diluted the average cost of electricity for captive residential users. Growth paid for itself.

By the early 1970s, this physical dynamic halted. The thermal efficiency of steam turbines struck thermodynamic ceilings, environmental mandates required capital-intensive cooling and flue gas scrubbers, and general inflation drove nominal borrowing costs to historic highs. For the first time in commercial utility history, the industry entered an era of increasing marginal costs. Adding a new megawatt of peak capacity was now significantly more expensive than the embedded average cost of the existing generating fleet.

Madison Gas and Electric Company, an investor-owned utility serving southern Wisconsin, sought a general rate increase using traditional rolled-in accounting, seeking to maintain its declining block tariffs. But in a proceeding presided over by Chairman Richard Cudahy, two civic intervenors—Capitol Community Citizens and the Environmental Defense Fund, deploying economists Charles Cicchetti and William Baumol—formally contested the utility’s Cost of Service Study (COSS).

The legal contest centered on the statutory standard embedded in American public utility law: rates must be "just, reasonable, and not unduly preferential or unjustly discriminatory." Intervenors demonstrated that when long-run marginal costs exceed historical embedded costs, traditional accounting creates an invisible, regressive wealth transfer. Under rolled-in pricing, captive residential consumers—whose demand was inelastic and comparatively flat—were forced to pay an artificial premium to finance the high-voltage transmission lines, substations, and peaking generation built solely to satisfy the expanding load of commercial and industrial customers.

The Wisconsin Commission’s August 1974 order established what became known across regulatory jurisprudence as the "cost causation principle" tied to Long-Run Incremental Cost (LRIC). The commission ruled that declining block rates were unlawful because they failed to reflect true cost causation at the margin. When an industrial user’s expanding demand forces a utility to procure new, higher-cost generating and transmission assets, that specific customer class must be assigned the marginal capacity costs through peak-load pricing, time-differentiated rates, and separate demand charges, rather than socializing the expense across the general rate base.

Yet the Madison Gas and Electric doctrine encountered an immediate mathematical paradox born of statutory design. Under the US Supreme Court’s foundational ruling in Federal Power Commission v. Hope Natural Gas Co. (1944), a regulated utility’s total revenue is strictly capped at its prudently incurred costs plus a fair rate of return on its net capital assets. In an era of increasing marginal costs, if a commission charged large expanding loads their true, full marginal cost of service, the utility’s aggregate collections would exceed its constitutional revenue requirement, generating an illegal monopoly windfall.

To resolve this surplus without underpricing marginal demand, regulators were forced into complex administrative compromises. They relied on mechanical cost-allocation conventions—such as the Coincident Peak (CP) method and the Average and Excess Demand method—to divide joint transmission and generation costs among customer classes.

This administrative compromise transformed the regulatory docket into a venue of high technical capture. Large industrial consumers retained specialized regulatory counsel and forensic rate engineers to contest these allocation models, successfully carving out bespoke tariff schedules: interruptible power credits, off-peak load exemptions, and dedicated wholesale transmission tariffs that exempted them from paying for the broader grid upgrades their aggregate growth required. The formal legal standard of "cost causation" remained enshrined in statute, but its evidentiary enforcement was systematically diluted into negotiable accounting formulas, leaving captive residential consumers structurally vulnerable to bearing the fixed capital costs of an expanding grid.
