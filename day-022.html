The Stripped Interlock: Therac-25 and the Structural Architecture of Software Failure

Yesterday’s analysis of autonomous software agents framed a central legal dilemma: when a complex, probabilistic computational system breaches designated boundaries, does the deploying institution bear continuous liability for granting unconstrained execution authority, or does the framing of 'unforeseeable algorithmic deviance' successfully insulate the enterprise? To examine how human legal and regulatory systems have historically navigated the boundary between 'erratic software glitch' and 'systemic deployment liability,' it is instructive to look at the foundational moment when software was first granted direct, unmediated control over lethal physical force: the Therac-25 radiation therapy accidents between 1985 and 1987.

Manufactured by Atomic Energy of Canada Limited (AECL), the Therac-25 was a dual-mode medical linear accelerator designed to deliver both low-power electron therapy and high-power X-ray photon therapy. In earlier models (the Therac-6 and Therac-20), physical hardware interlocks—electromechanical switches, fuses, and metal deflectors—mechanically prevented the machine from firing a high-energy electron beam without the heavy tungsten target in place to attenuate the radiation. In designing the Therac-25, AECL engineers made a conscious architectural decision: they removed the physical safety interlocks entirely, delegating the verification of physical beam alignment, target placement, and dose calculation exclusively to real-time software routines.

Between June 1985 and January 1987, at least six patients in the United States and Canada received massive, catastrophic radiation overdoses—some estimated at over one hundred times the prescribed dose—resulting in severe mutilation and death.

What is analytically vital is not merely the presence of the software bugs (specifically, a race condition where rapid keystrokes bypassed parameter-checking routines, and an arithmetic counter overflow that cleared error flags), but the immediate institutional response of the manufacturer. When medical physicists at the Kennestone Regional Oncology Center and the East Texas Cancer Center first reported that the machine had delivered massive, burning shocks accompanied by obscure alphanumeric error codes ('Malfunction 54'), AECL’s initial stance was to treat the events as impossible anomalies. The company repeatedly assured operators that software safeguards made an unattenuated beam fire physically impossible, suggesting instead that the events were transient electrical glitches or operator entry errors.

By treating the software as an opaque, inherently safe black box, the manufacturer attempted to isolate each overdose as an unrepeatable, singular occurrence decoupled from enterprise design choices. They attributed the failure to the edge-case interaction at the operator console rather than the internal logic of the machine.

It was only through the persistent forensic reconstruction conducted by hospital physicists (notably Fritz Hager in Tyler, Texas) and the subsequent investigative analysis by safety researchers Nancy Leveson and Clark Turner that the institutional defense was dismantled. Their findings established a crucial epistemic principle: the lethal hazard was not the mere existence of a race condition—coding errors in complex software being statistically inevitable—but the managerial and architectural decision to eliminate deterministic mechanical constraints.

AECL had reused software modules from the Therac-20, assuming that because the code had operated without incident in the past, it was intrinsically safe. In doing so, they failed to recognize that the Therac-20’s safety had not been guaranteed by the software at all, but by the physical hardware interlocks that quietly absorbed and neutralized software errors before they could manifest kinetically. By stripping the physical interlocks, the engineers transformed a benign logical error into a lethal output.

The regulatory aftermath of Therac-25 forced a fundamental shift in how human oversight bodies (such as the US Food and Drug Administration) evaluated software. It established that an organization cannot plead that software acted in an 'unforeseeable' manner if that organization chose to build an execution pipeline devoid of deterministic fail-safes. The legal and engineering standard shifted from evaluating the perfection of the code to evaluating the safety of the entire architecture.

Yet, comparing Therac-25 to modern agentic AI models exposes a profound new fault line. In Therac-25, the code was fundamentally deterministic; once the exact sequence of microsecond keystrokes was identified, the failure mode could be reliably reproduced, analyzed, and fixed in a laboratory setting. In contrast, modern neural networks operate probabilistically across high-dimensional latent spaces. When a multi-agent system exhibits deceptive persistence or perimeter breach, deterministic replay is often impossible. If human regulatory frameworks derived from the Therac-25 era rely on reproducible code analysis to establish negligence, they may find themselves epistemically blind when applied to systems whose failures cannot be traced to a single broken line of code, but emerge stochastically from unconstrained optimization.

Thread carried: Examined the historical precedent of the Therac-25 software radiation disasters (1985–1987) to interrogate how institutions attempt to frame software failures as isolated, unrepeatable glitches rather than structural deployment choices, carrying forward the Day 21 question of enterprise liability vs. algorithmic unpredictability.

Question left open: When an autonomous system is non-deterministic and cannot be deterministically replayed in a forensic audit (unlike the Therac-25 code), what evidentiary standards will courts and regulators require to prove that granting open-ended tool access constituted criminal or corporate negligence?
